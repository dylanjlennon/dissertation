A PhD story · Nutritional Sciences · University of Florida

Food changes the brain. We don't know how.

For children with epilepsy that medication can't control, a ketogenic diet can quiet seizures. My PhD asked what we were missing between the plate and the brain, by measuring diet and the gut microbiome in far higher resolution.

Prologue

Three links, two of them blurry

For more than 90 years, doctors have treated epilepsy with food. Remove most of the carbohydrate, replace it with fat, and seizures change.
In children whose seizures medication can't control, the diet dramatically improves seizures in about a third, helps another third, and does little for the rest. No one knows why.Dissertation Ch. 1
Food is the medicine here, so something in the food is doing the work. Yet the diet is prescribed and studied as a single number: the ratio of fat to protein plus carbohydrate.
Between the plate and the brain sits the gut microbiome. Diet reshapes gut microbes, microbes change how diet affects metabolism, and they make signaling molecules such as GABA and serotonin. When I began, their role in this therapy was completely unexplored.
And when the gut microbiome is studied at all, it is read from one place: stool.
So my dissertation set out to sharpen the two blurry links. Diet, resolved to the molecule. The gut, mapped site by site. The brain stays the open question, but now there is something precise to connect it to.
Part one · The first link

Diet is not just diet

Start with the medicine itself: the food. Most nutrition science records a food as a few numbers off a label, and the ketogenic diet is prescribed from those same few numbers.
Fat, protein, carbohydrate. The ketogenic ratio divides the first by the other two, as if every gram of fat were the same.
But fat is dozens of fatty acids. Protein is twenty amino acids. Carbohydrate is sugars, starches and fiber. I built foodomics to see them: a database linking 2,731 brand-name foods to 8,789 USDA molecular profiles.17,363 profiles from 8,557 label collections, 1997 to 2016. Ch. 3
Then I found that food doesn't hold still. Of 964 foods we tracked over time, 46.5% changed their label at least once. Carbohydrate changed most often.1,411 changes in all. Tables 3-1 and 3-2
In one modeled meal, swapping between equivalent brands at identical gram weights doubled the carbohydrate. For a child on ketogenic therapy, a carbohydrate spike can mean a breakthrough seizure.Burrito meal model, brand set A vs. B. Figure 3-13
Part two

Same prescription, different diet

I brought foodomics into the clinic. UF's precision ketogenic therapy program, where I spent five years on the nutrition therapy staff, follows children whose seizures medication couldn't control.
8,999days of brand-specific intake from 53 children, some records spanning up to 20 years, each day resolved to the molecule.
The therapy is prescribed as one number, a ratio of fat to protein plus carbohydrate. But at every ratio, the kind of fat children actually ate varied on its own. Same prescription, different diet. And since children respond so differently to the same ratio, those hidden pixels are where to look.Fat saturation profiles by ratio stratum and feeding route. Figures 4-2 to 4-7
Part three · The middle link

Going upstream

Now the middle link. To see what diet does to gut microbes, scientists almost always look at the same thing: a stool sample. It's painless to collect, and it's what at-home microbiome tests are built on.
But stool comes from the very end of the gut. The small intestine, where most nutrients are absorbed and the immune system gets much of its education, only reaches us secondhand.
So I went upstream, and started at birth, because our team believed ketogenic therapy may work better when started early in life. In a piglet neonatal intensive care unit, a translational model of the human newborn, I sampled 13 points along the whole intestine: three in each third of the small intestine and four along the colon.
At every point I took two samples: what flows through the gut, the lumen, and the lining itself, the mucosa. Twenty-six sites per animal, each a different neighborhood.
288samples from six pairs of littermates, one sampled at 1 to 2 days old and one at 9 to 10 days old, read with 8.8 million 16S rRNA sequences.Dissertation Ch. 2, Tables 2-1 and 2-2
Part four

A map forms in ten days

The first surprise: in day-old piglets, it barely mattered where in the gut I looked. Location explained just 5.6% of the differences between microbial communities.
Eight days later, location explained a third. In just over a week, the gut had sorted its microbes into neighborhoods.PERMANOVA on unweighted UniFrac distances, 999 permutations. Tables 2-6 to 2-8
And the neighborhoods are distinct. Firmicutes and Proteobacteria crowd the small intestine. Bacteroidetes, almost absent at birth, has claimed the colon.Mean relative abundance, lumen, day 9 to 10. Table 2-9
Part five

The twist

Which raises the question that matters to anyone reading a stool test: does stool still reflect what's happening upstream?
In the youngest piglets, mostly yes. For microbes found in both places, stool abundances predicted the small intestine with an R² of 0.67, and the colon with 0.70.
By day 10, that link had collapsed. Stool explained 7% of the variation in the small intestine's contents, and essentially none on its lining.Linear regression of shared OTU abundances, site vs. stool. Figure 2-14
The same ten days that build the gut's map are the days stool stops being a faithful copy of it. Stool still reads the colon's contents. It reads the rest of the gut less and less. If the microbes that respond to a diet live upstream, stool alone may never show them.
Back to the question

Diet is not just diet. Stool is not the whole gut.

Food changes the brain in these children, and the path may run through the gut. My dissertation built the tools to see both links in high resolution: diet resolved to the molecule, and the gut mapped site by site from its first days of life.

The year after I finished

A team at UCLA showed in mice that the ketogenic diet's protection against seizures disappears without gut bacteria, and comes back when two bacteria the diet enriches, Akkermansia and Parabacteroides, are restored together. Those microbes shifted the brain's balance of GABA to glutamate. The middle link is real.

Olson CA, Vuong HE, Yano JM, Liang QY, Nusbaum DJ, Hsiao EY. The gut microbiota mediates the anti-seizure effects of the ketogenic diet. Cell. 2018;173(7):1728-1741. doi:10.1016/j.cell.2018.04.027

Work like this usually reads the gut from stool and defines the diet by its formula. The next step needs both in high resolution, which is what my tools were built for: which food molecules, acting through which microbes, in which part of the gut, change the brain? Children respond differently to the same ratio. The answer may be in the pixels.

Sequencing and processing

  • 16S rRNA V1 to V3, Illumina MiSeq
  • Q > 30 quality filter; FLASH read joining
  • QIIME 1.8 open-reference OTUs at 97%, GreenGenes 13_8
  • UCHIME chimera removal; UF HiPerGator

Normalization and statistics

  • Occupancy classification as a biological filter
  • Site-level geometric-mean normalization (DESeq2) instead of rarefying
  • UniFrac and Bray-Curtis; PERMANOVA and PERMDISP (vegan); PCoA (phyloseq)
  • Kruskal-Wallis, Benjamini-Hochberg; R 3.3.1

Foodomics

  • Nutrition Facts Labels linked to USDA SR28 seed profiles, then adjusted to label values
  • Versioned MySQL database
  • Ternary plots, PCA and PCoA of molecular profiles; 365-day brand-switching model
