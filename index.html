What a stool sample can't tell you.

I spent my PhD trying to see the newborn gut and the food that feeds it in higher resolution. Here is what turned up, told with the data.

Scroll to begin
Part one

Going upstream

When scientists want to know what lives in a baby's gut, they almost always look at the same thing: a stool sample. It's painless to collect, and it's what at-home microbiome tests are built on.
But stool comes from the very end of the gut. The small intestine, where most nutrients are absorbed and the immune system gets much of its education, only reaches us secondhand.
So I went upstream. In a piglet neonatal intensive care unit, a translational model of the human newborn, I sampled 13 points along the whole intestine: three in each third of the small intestine and four along the colon.
At every point I took two samples: what flows through the gut, the lumen, and the lining itself, the mucosa. Twenty-six sites per animal, each a different neighborhood.
288samples from six pairs of littermates, one sampled at 1 to 2 days old and one at 9 to 10 days old, read with 8.8 million 16S rRNA sequences.Dissertation Ch. 2, Tables 2-1 and 2-2
Part two

A map forms in ten days

The first surprise: in day-old piglets, it barely mattered where in the gut I looked. Location explained just 5.6% of the differences between microbial communities.
Eight days later, location explained a third. In just over a week, the gut had sorted its microbes into neighborhoods.PERMANOVA on unweighted UniFrac distances, 999 permutations. Tables 2-6 to 2-8
And the neighborhoods are distinct. Firmicutes and Proteobacteria crowd the small intestine. Bacteroidetes, almost absent at birth, has claimed the colon.Mean relative abundance, lumen, day 9 to 10. Table 2-9
Part three

The twist

Which raises the question that matters to anyone reading a stool test: does stool still reflect what's happening upstream?
In the youngest piglets, mostly yes. For microbes found in both places, stool abundances predicted the small intestine with an R² of 0.67, and the colon with 0.70.
By day 10, that link had collapsed. Stool explained 7% of the variation in the small intestine's contents, and essentially none on its lining.Linear regression of shared OTU abundances, site vs. stool. Figure 2-14
The same ten days that build the gut's map are the days stool stops being a faithful copy of it. Stool still reads the colon's contents. It reads the rest of the gut less and less.
Part four

The other side of the gut

Everything a gut microbe eats, it gets from us. And diet has the same resolution problem. Most nutrition science records a food as a few numbers off a label.
Fat, protein, carbohydrate. That's where most diet records stop.
But fat is dozens of fatty acids. Protein is twenty amino acids. Carbohydrate is sugars, starches and fiber. I built foodomics to see them: a database linking 2,731 brand-name foods to 8,789 USDA molecular profiles.17,363 profiles from 8,557 label collections, 1997 to 2016. Ch. 3
Then I found that food doesn't hold still. Of 964 foods we tracked over time, 46.5% changed their label at least once. Carbohydrate changed most often.1,411 changes in all. Tables 3-1 and 3-2
In one modeled meal, swapping between equivalent brands at identical gram weights doubled the carbohydrate. For a child on ketogenic therapy, a carbohydrate spike can mean a breakthrough seizure.Burrito meal model, brand set A vs. B. Figure 3-13
Part five

Same prescription, different diet

I brought foodomics into the clinic. UF's precision ketogenic therapy program, where I spent five years on the nutrition therapy staff, follows children whose seizures medication couldn't control.
8,999days of brand-specific intake from 53 children, some records spanning up to 20 years, each day resolved to the molecule.
The therapy is prescribed as one number, a ratio of fat to protein plus carbohydrate. But at every ratio, the kind of fat children actually ate varied on its own. Same prescription, different diet.Fat saturation profiles by ratio stratum and feeding route. Figures 4-2 to 4-7
What it adds up to

Higher resolution on both sides of the gut.

What goes in, measured to the molecule. What lives inside, mapped site by site. And a reminder that the first days of life move fast: the window when the gut builds its map is the window when a simple sample starts to miss it.

What I'd bring

Longitudinal study design, compositional microbiome statistics in R, clean relational data built to last, and the habit of asking what a measurement can and can't see before trusting it.

Where I want to work

Early-life microbiome and nutrition data, followed over time and explained clearly to the families and clinicians who depend on it.

Sequencing and processing

  • 16S rRNA V1 to V3, Illumina MiSeq
  • Q > 30 quality filter; FLASH read joining
  • QIIME 1.8 open-reference OTUs at 97%, GreenGenes 13_8
  • UCHIME chimera removal; UF HiPerGator

Normalization and statistics

  • Occupancy classification as a biological filter
  • Site-level geometric-mean normalization (DESeq2) instead of rarefying
  • UniFrac and Bray-Curtis; PERMANOVA and PERMDISP (vegan); PCoA (phyloseq)
  • Kruskal-Wallis, Benjamini-Hochberg; R 3.3.1

Foodomics

  • Nutrition Facts Labels linked to USDA SR28 seed profiles, then adjusted to label values
  • Versioned MySQL database
  • Ternary plots, PCA and PCoA of molecular profiles; 365-day brand-switching model
